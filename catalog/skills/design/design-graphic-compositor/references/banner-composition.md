## 1. Start with communication and environment

Record before sketching:

- physical width and height;
- viewing direction and typical distance;
- whether viewers walk past or stop;
- eye level, furniture, counters, doors, screens, seams, and mounting hardware;
- required copy and mandatory marks;
- whether the banner is atmospheric, informational, directional, or transitional.

An atmospheric surface can prioritize rhythm and edge continuity. An informational surface must prioritize reading order. A transitional surface must visually explain the relationship between adjacent zones.

## 2. Aspect-ratio behavior

Use ratios as tendencies, not templates:

- **Panoramic, above 2.5:1:** emphasize lateral movement, repeated rhythm, edge framing, or a long visual sentence. Avoid a small centered island.
- **Wide, 1.6-2.5:1:** use asymmetric thirds, a strong content block plus image field, edge-to-center framing, or a controlled central stage.
- **Near-square, 0.85-1.6:1:** use a central stack, bilateral transition, diagonal tension, or an upper focal mark with a grounded lower scene.
- **Tall, below 0.85:1:** build a vertical sequence and protect the lower obstruction zone.

Test optical balance, not mathematical symmetry. Dense detail, bright colors, faces, and high contrast carry more visual weight than their area suggests.

## 3. Composition models

### A. Pattern field

Use for background-only banners and low-information environmental surfaces.

- Establish a deliberate pattern scale relative to the human body and panel size.
- Avoid accidental tangencies or obviously clipped motifs near seams.
- Create one density rhythm: uniform, edge-weighted, or gently directional.
- Do not add a fake focal point unless the brief needs one.

### B. Editorial anchor

Use for consultation, information, or navigation zones.

- Reserve one stable copy block with strong contrast.
- Pair it with one supporting image/shape field rather than many equal illustrations.
- Keep reading order: zone identity → promise/function → secondary guidance → partner marks.
- Protect the copy from furniture and traffic sight lines.

### C. Central stage

Use when one official mark or short statement is the primary content.

- Give the mark generous protected negative space.
- Build framing elements toward it without forming a blank moat.
- Offset optical center when the lower scene or nearby architecture adds weight.

### D. Bilateral transition

Use between two zones, districts, or themes.

- Give left and right distinct vocabularies while sharing background, baseline, atmospheric treatment, and mid-zone overlap.
- Lower density near the central mark or transition phrase.
- Avoid a hard 50/50 split unless separation is the message.
- Use bridge elements—neutral forms, trajectory, color migration, or overlapping silhouettes—to make the relationship legible.

### E. Edge-weighted proscenium

Use for skylines, landscapes, or architectural frames around a central message.

- Keep taller/darker masses near outer edges.
- Step down toward the focal region.
- Connect the lower edge with overlaps or a shared baseline.
- Prevent the scene from becoming a pasted footer.

### F. Diagonal flow

Use for energy, movement, careers, or process themes.

- Choose one entry and one exit direction.
- Align crops, pattern, and trajectory to the same vector.
- Keep diagonal decoration behind protected copy and marks.

## 4. Building a banner series

Define shared tokens before individual layouts:

- background family and gradient behavior;
- outer safe area and internal spacing unit;
- logo zone and relative logo scales;
- baseline/corner treatment;
- pattern stroke/scale/density;
- image grade, cutout edge, shadow, and grain rules;
- headline sizes and line-height system.

Vary composition according to function. Repeat the system, not the exact arrangement.

Create a strip proof showing every banner at equal visual height and at approximate installed adjacency. Check rhythm, color jumps, duplicated focal points, and seam interactions.

## 5. Information hierarchy and distance

Limit hierarchy to three primary levels whenever possible:

1. recognition from far distance;
2. key message from middle distance;
3. supporting details at close distance.

For each level, test the proof at the corresponding apparent size. Do not rely only on document zoom percentage. Shorten copy before shrinking it below useful legibility.

Use line breaks intentionally. Avoid orphans, accidental logo-like text stacks, and long centered paragraphs. Prefer ragged text blocks for information-heavy copy unless the brandbook explicitly favors centered display typography.

## 6. Exhibition-specific checks

- Mark the hidden/obstructed lower band caused by counters and people.
- Keep faces, QR codes, essential copy, and logo clearspace away from panel seams and hardware.
- Check whether a seam passes through a high-contrast edge or letter.
- Inspect continuation between neighboring panels at installed scale.
- Provide bleed and wrap only according to the fabricator's method.
- Remember that dark large fields, gradients, and near-neutrals expose banding and print inconsistency.

## 7. Iteration protocol

1. Make 2-3 materially different block compositions.
2. Select using hierarchy, function, and environment—not decorative finish.
3. Lock major occupied/quiet regions.
4. Integrate final imagery and typography.
5. Correct one class of problem per pass: hierarchy, balance, integration, detail, production.
6. Recheck the whole series after every major individual change.
