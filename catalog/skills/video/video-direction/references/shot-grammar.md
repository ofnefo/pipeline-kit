# Shot grammar: what each shot is for

Use this while you write `00-brief/shotlist.md`. A shot list is a chain of decisions, and each row has to say why it exists.
Pick sizes, angles and staging by the job the shot does. Do not pick them because they look "cinematic".

## 1. Every shot does a job

A shot earns its place only if it does at least one of these three things. If it does none of them, cut it or merge it.

- **Changes the emotion.** The viewer feels differently after it than before it.
- **Moves the action forward.** Something happens that cannot be undone.
- **Raises the pressure.** The time, the space or the options get smaller.

Name the job in one word in the shot-list row (the `function` column):

| Function | What it does | Typical size / move |
|---|---|---|
| Establish | Shows where we are and who is where | Wide or extreme wide, static or a slow move |
| Power | Shows who controls the scene | Low angle, standing over a seated person, holding a doorway |
| Pressure | Shows the walls closing in | Tight frame, a slow push-in, a crowded foreground |
| Detail | Shows the object or gesture that matters | Insert or macro, static |
| Reaction | Shows how a person takes in what just happened | Close-up, held a beat longer than feels comfortable |
| Shift | Shows that a decision or new information lands | A rack focus, a change of size, or a move that starts |
| Impact | Shows the hit, reveal or turn | The shortest cut in the sequence, or a hard stop |
| Aftermath | Shows what is left | Wide and still; let the sound carry it |
| Exit | Shows the release or the hand-off to the next scene | A pull-back, or someone walking out of frame |

## 2. Shot size and angle: what they mean

| Size | What it gives the viewer |
|---|---|
| Extreme wide / wide | Geography and scale; the person is small against the place |
| Full / medium wide | The whole body: posture, walk, distance between people |
| Medium | Gesture and dialogue, the working default |
| Medium close / close-up | Thought and reaction; the face carries the scene |
| Extreme close / insert | A single detail that the story turns on |

| Angle | What it gives the viewer |
|---|---|
| Eye level | Neutral; we stand with the person |
| Low | Strength, threat, monumentality |
| High | Smallness, exposure, being watched |
| Overhead | Pattern, fate, a map of the situation |
| Dutch (tilted) | Instability. Use it rarely and only with a reason |
| Over the shoulder (OTS) | A relationship; whose shoulder we are behind tells us whose side we are on |
| Point of view (POV) | We see as the person sees; follow it with a reaction shot |

Write the camera height in metres (for example "camera 0.9 m, below eye line") and not "low angle". A height in metres
stays the same from shot to shot. A word does not.

## 3. Geography and axis

- **Geography sentence.** Before the first shot, write one sentence about the space: who stands where, where the exits are,
  where the light comes from. Example: "Kitchen; she is at the window (screen left), he blocks the door (screen right),
  morning sun from the window."
- **180° rule.** Keep the camera on one side of the line between two people, so screen left and screen right stay put. To
  cross the line, show the move on camera, or cut through a neutral shot taken on the axis.
- **Screen direction.** A person who leaves frame right must come into the next shot from frame left. A journey keeps
  one direction across the whole sequence. Reverse it only on purpose (a retreat, a return).
- **Eyeline.** Work out where a person looks from their height and from where the other person stands. Write it as a
  direction ("looks up-right, 30° off lens"), so the matching reverse shot lines up.
- **30° / two-size rule.** Between two cuts on the same subject, move the camera at least 30° or change the size by at
  least two steps (for example wide to medium close). Anything smaller reads as a jump cut.

## 4. Staging: who holds the power

Plan where people stand and how they move as **desire**, not decoration. For each person write:

1. who they are in this shot;
2. where they start;
3. where they end;
4. what object they touch;
5. what they look at;
6. what the body gives away that the words do not.

How bodies move, and what it tells the viewer:

- Moving toward someone: wanting, attacking, confessing.
- Moving away: fear, refusal, shame.
- Being pushed into a corner, or into the edge of frame: being trapped.
- Yielding space, sitting down, turning the back: giving up control.
- One small revealing gesture is worth more than a line of dialogue.

What the frame says about power:

- Standing over someone seated.
- Holding a doorway.
- Seen through glass or in a reflection.
- Part of the face in shadow.
- A lot of empty space around a small figure.
- A tight frame with no headroom.
- Two people in one frame who do not look at each other.

## 5. Editing priorities

When two goals pull against each other in a cut, keep this order:

1. emotion;
2. story;
3. rhythm;
4. where the eye is looking;
5. the flat composition of the frame;
6. continuity of the 3D space.

A cut that is right for the emotion beats a cut that is right for continuity. This is a classic editing priority list.
Its often-quoted weights are roughly 51 / 23 / 10 / 7 / 5 / 4 %.

## 6. Shot-list row: the minimum fields

These add to the fields in `00-brief/shotlist.md`. `sequence-dramaturgy.md` has the full shot card.

`id | function | size + angle + height | move (see camera-moves.md) | action, start to end state | first / last frame |
duration | sound cue | continuity notes (see continuity-bible.md)`

Checks before the list goes to previz ([previz.md](previz.md)):

- Each shot has a function from section 1, and no two shots in a row repeat the same function without a reason.
- There are no more than two close-ups on the same axis in a row.
- The geography sentence exists, and every shot respects the axis and the screen direction.
- The sequence keeps at least one wide shot to re-orient the viewer every 4–6 shots.
